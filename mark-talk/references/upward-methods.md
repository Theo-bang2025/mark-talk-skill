# Upward communication methods

Select one or two relevant units. These are internal thinking aids, not fixed scripts. Adapt order, detail and voice to the user's relationship, medium and goal; a short message can omit most elements. Examples below are original fictional illustrations in English, not measured outputs or text to translate mechanically. Respond in the user's language. Use only facts actually supplied in the real task.

## Quick selection

| Need | Unit | Main question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Show work | Progress | What is done, observed and still open? |
| Get unstuck | Help | What assistance is needed now? |
| Seek approval | Recommendation | What do I recommend, on what basis? |
| Interpret vague feedback | Standards | What priority or example would guide the work? |
| Obtain support | Resources | What resource covers what gap? |
| State a limit | Constraints | What is actually infeasible or unaccepted? |
| Address criticism | Criticism | What is verified, disputed or unclear? |
| Close a loop | Confirmation | What has actually been agreed? |

## 1. Progress: make work visible

Use for updates and recognition. Need actual outputs/actions, status, and any reported feedback. Select the evidence that answers the recipient's question; distinguish completion from testing and impact. Add an open issue or proposed next step only if relevant and supported. Sources: SBAR and SBI.

- Given: a guide is finished, two colleagues tried it, feedback has not arrived. Example: "The guide is finished and two colleagues have tried it. I'm still waiting for their feedback."
- Misuse: "The guide has been validated and is ready to roll out." Trying something does not establish quality or readiness. If useful, ask what the trials revealed.

## 2. Help: ask with a clear point of need

Use when blocked or urgent assistance matters. Need the actual problem and requested support; include prior attempts only when supplied. State the impact if known, then ask for something answerable. Urgency can justify asking before investigation; preparation is not an admission ticket to help. Source: SBAR.

- Given: an account is inaccessible before a demo; no diagnosis yet. Example: "The demo account won't let me in. Could you help find someone with access to check it?"
- Misuse: "I've ruled out all local causes." Do not invent diligence, blame or a repair estimate.

## 3. Recommendation: contribute judgment before seeking a decision

Use when the user has enough evidence to propose a course and the recipient owns approval or a tradeoff. Need options or a stated recommendation and its actual reason. Lead with the recommendation when that fits the recipient's known preference. Clarify if the basis is missing. Sources: SBAR and PON.

- Given: a wording fix takes a day; the current pack is usable and needed today; user recommends sending. Example: "I suggest sending the usable pack for today's session and correcting the wording afterward. Holding it for the edit would miss this session. Do you agree?"
- Misuse: "The launch plan is entirely up to you." Do not hand over the user's own responsibilities or assume the recipient's authority. An A/B menu is optional, not universal.

## 4. Standards: narrow the uncertainty

Use for vague instructions or feedback. Need the actual wording and what remains unclear. Ask for a priority, representative location or acceptance criterion; ask about timing when it changes the work. Offer possible directions as hypotheses, keeping room for another answer. Sources: SBI/SBII and SBAR.

- Given: a mentor says "make the proposal stronger" without a deadline. Example: "Which part most needs strengthening? A specific example would help me understand the priority. When do you need the revision?"
- Misuse: "You must mean the data is weak; I'll meet you tomorrow for ten minutes." Neither diagnosis nor availability follows from vague feedback. Avoid long classification menus.

## 5. Resources: connect the request to the real gap

Use for staffing, access, time or other support. Need requested resource/duration, its purpose and the known capacity gap. Use real preparation as evidence; ask about the gap when it would materially strengthen the request. A concise initial request can still work with limited information. Sources: SBAR and PON.

- Given: the registration form is ready, help is needed with inquiries, the user requests one operator for two weeks. Example: "Could we arrange one operations colleague for two weeks to handle registration inquiries? The form is ready; this is the support I need for the next stage."
- Misuse: "Even half-time for one week is fine, and I can fit their schedule." Do not invent acceptable concessions, workload figures or risk probabilities. Discuss unaccepted alternatives without promising execution.

## 6. Constraints: be respectful and precise

Use when a requested scope, time or arrangement is infeasible or unaccepted. Need the user's exact limit and any alternative they can and want to offer. State the constraint plainly; clarify the underlying need when useful. An alternative is optional. Sources: DESC and PON concessions.

- Given: away without a computer; cannot redo the whole report tonight; other availability unknown. Example: "I'm away without my computer, so I can't redo the whole report tonight. Which changes are most important, and when is the revised report needed?"
- Misuse: "I'm available on my phone all evening" or "I can't do anything this weekend." Neither follows. If the user says no contact tonight, do not add a call, text review or progress update. Power differences matter; wording cannot guarantee a safe reaction.

## 7. Criticism or misunderstanding: separate verified responsibility from inference

Use for negative feedback or a disputed account. Need actual criticism, verified facts, and what the user wants to acknowledge or clarify. Address a real mistake without inventing broader fault. For a misunderstanding, explain the relevant fact and seek correction or a standard; avoid diagnosing motives. Sources: SBI/SBII and DESC.

- Given: two cited years are wrong; other citations unchecked. Example: "I checked the two references you flagged; both years are wrong. Beyond those errors, is there a particular issue in the argument you want me to focus on?"
- Misuse: "I've checked everything else and it's correct" or "You're only saying this because you dislike me." An apology or a correction promise should not expand responsibility or accepted work.

## 8. Confirmation and follow-up: close only the actual loop

Use after a real decision or when new status justifies an update. Need the agreed scope, responsibilities and any accepted timing. Repeat only what matters; leave unresolved matters open. Source: Check-back.

- Given: user proposed sending an outline Friday; supervisor agreed to that and said to defer the appendix. Example: "Understood: the outline on Friday, with the appendix deferred."
- Misuse: "I'll also send a full draft tomorrow morning." Confirmation is not permission to add work. No new information may mean no follow-up message is needed.

## Sources and limits

Reviewed 2026-10-21. These institutional teaching frameworks inform original adaptations; they do not establish effectiveness in Chinese upward communication or for Mark Talk. Runtime use requires no website or book access.

- [AHRQ SBAR](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/sbar.html): situation, relevant background, assessment and recommendation/request in healthcare teams. We select useful elements rather than require the full medical script.
- [CCL SBI/SBII](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/closing-the-gap-between-intent-vs-impact-sbii/): distinguish observable behavior, impact and intent inquiry. Do not infer impact or intent from an action alone.
- [AHRQ DESC](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/mutual/tools/desc.html): describe concerns, suggest alternatives and discuss consequences; timing, privacy and organizational culture affect use.
- [AHRQ Check-back](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/checkback.html): repeat information and verify shared understanding; do not manufacture agreement.
- [Harvard PON principled negotiation](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/tag/principled-negotiation/): interests, options and objective criteria. Do not assume a particular manager's interests.
- [Harvard PON concessions](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/four-strategies-for-making-concessions/): make a real concession explicit; conditional concessions are situational. User acceptance comes before including one in their message.
